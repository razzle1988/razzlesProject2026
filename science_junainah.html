How Receptors and Nerves Work

Every second, your body is collecting information: light bouncing off this page, the hum of a fridge, the smell of dinner cooking. Your body collects this information using five sense organs: eyes (sight), ears (hearing), nose (smell), tongue (taste), and skin (touch).

Each sense organ contains receptors, tiny structures that detect a specific kind of information, such as light, sound waves, chemicals, or pressure. When a receptor is triggered, it sends an electrical signal along a nerve. Nerves are part of your nervous system, a network that carries messages around your body at incredible speed, up to about 120 metres per second.

All these signals travel to your brain, which interprets them and decides how to respond. Sometimes the response is a thought ("that smells like popcorn"), and sometimes it's an automatic reaction called a reflex, like blinking or pulling your hand off something hot before you've even consciously felt the pain.

💡
DID YOU KNOW? A reflex reaction can happen before your brain has even finished processing the pain! Some reflex signals only travel as far as your spinal cord and back, which is faster than sending a message all the way to the brain.

🧪 Activity: Reflex and Reaction Lab & Eye Experiment

Part 1 (Hand Reaction): Sit facing a partner. Have them hold their hand out, palm up, just under yours without touching. Without warning, they try to catch your hand as you snatch it away. Swap roles.

Part 2 (Knee Reflex): Gently tap just below the kneecap with the side of your hand while your leg dangles freely. Watch the automatic leg twitch!

Part 3 (Eye Mirror Experiment): In a dim room, look at your eyes in a mirror and note the pupil size. Switch on a bright light and watch again!

👂 Hearing

Sound travels through air as waves. The outer ear funnels waves to the eardrum, vibrating three tiny bones that pass movement to the fluid-filled, snail-shaped cochlea, where receptors create nerve signals.

👅 Smell & Taste

Work as a team called chemoreception. Smell receptors detect airborne particles. Taste buds detect sweet, sour, salty, bitter, and umami. Most "flavour" is actually smell!

✋ Touch

Skin is the body's largest organ! It houses receptors for light pressure, deep pressure, temperature, and pain. Fingertips and lips have the highest receptor density.

The MRS GREN Acronym (Memory Trick)

Scientists use 7 characteristics to decide if something is alive. It must do ALL seven!

M
Movement Living things move all or part of themselves (even plants turning to light).
R
Respiration Releasing energy from food inside cells (not just breathing!).
S
Sensitivity Detecting and responding to changes in surroundings (stimuli).
G
Growth Getting bigger and developing over a lifetime.
R
Reproduction Producing offspring, more of their own kind.
E
Excretion Removing waste products made inside their bodies.
N
Nutrition Taking in and using food (or making their own) for energy and building materials.

Living, Non-Living, or Dead? (3-Way Sorting)

🟢 Living

Currently doing all 7 MRS GREN characteristics right now. (e.g., a tree, a bird, a person)

🟡 Dead

Was once alive and could do all seven characteristics, but no longer can. (e.g., a fallen leaf, a dead insect, a cooked carrot)

⚪ Non-Living

Has never been alive and never could do the seven characteristics. (e.g., a rock, water, a toy car, a cloud)

Tricky Cases & Biological Debates

Case 1: A Dry Seed

A seed in a packet looks still and non-living. But inside, it is alive in a resting state called dormancy. Its life processes happen extremely slowly; with water, warmth, and light, it will sprout!

Case 2: A Virus

Viruses reproduce only by invading host cells. On their own, they cannot grow, respire, or respond. Because they fail most tests on their own, most scientists classify them as non-living.
